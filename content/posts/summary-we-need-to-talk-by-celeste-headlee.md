## Should You Read This?

**Toby's Rating: 8/10**

[We Need to Talk](https://www.amazon.com/dp/0062669011) by Celeste Headlee is the conversation book I give people who will not read a 400-page classic. She is a radio host. She interviews for a living. The TED talk — *10 ways to have a better conversation* — is the spine. The book is the flesh.

It will not replace [You’re Not Listening](/post/book-summary-you-re-not-listening-by-kate-murphy-big-ideas-and-best-quotes) or [How to Listen](/post/summary-how-to-listen-by-oscar-trimboli). Those go deeper. Headlee is the checklist I fail in ordinary meetings, not only the scary ones.

## Toby's Top Takeaway From We Need to Talk by Celeste Headlee

I pontificate.

I tell myself I am adding context. I am giving a lecture. Calvin Trillin called it a bore bomb. I wrote about that in [How to Know a Person](/post/summary-how-to-know-a-person-by-david-brooks). Headlee’s version is blunter. Enter every conversation assuming you have something to learn. If you do not, you are not in a conversation. You are on stage.

The rule I break next: I equate my experience with yours.

You tell me about a brutal restructure. I tell you about mine. I think I am connecting. I have stolen the ball. Headlee’s line is the one I now try to swallow: their story is not a prompt for yours.

This is hard for coaches and leaders. We are paid to pattern-match. “That reminds me of…” is often useful later. In the first two minutes it is a hijack.

The third habit. I listen to reply.

I can feel the sentence forming while they are still talking. My face is interested. My mind is in draft. Headlee would fail me. So would [Kate Murphy](/post/book-summary-you-re-not-listening-by-kate-murphy-big-ideas-and-best-quotes). So would Nancy Kline.

Her ten rules, the ones from the talk, are now a card in my notebook:

1. Don’t multitask. Be present or leave.
2. Don’t pontificate. Come to learn.
3. Use open-ended questions. Who, what, when, where, why, how.
4. Go with the flow. Let thoughts come and go. Don’t clutch the clever one.
5. If you don’t know, say so.
6. Don’t equate your experience with theirs.
7. Don’t repeat yourself. It’s boring and it is a power move.
8. Stay out of the weeds. Dates and names are for you, not them.
9. Listen.
10. Be brief.

Number nine is the whole book. Number ten is the one leaders hate. We add one more example. Then a caveat. Then a story from 2019. The point dies of kindness.

Headlee is also honest about phones. You cannot half-listen to a person and a screen. I still glance. They still see it. Respect, as [Crucial Conversations](/post/summary-crucial-conversations-by-kerry-patterson) says, is like air. When it is gone, it is all anyone thinks about.

If your team is tired of “alignment meetings” that are actually monologues, print the ten rules. Use them as a retro. I have. It is ugly. It is useful.

## 3 Big Ideas From We Need to Talk by Celeste Headlee

**Big Idea 1 — Conversation is a skill, and we are out of practice**

Headlee’s diagnosis is societal, not just managerial. We spend more time broadcasting. We sort into tribes. We lose the muscle of talking to someone we do not already agree with.

At work this looks like meetings where everyone performs their side. No one changes shape. [High Conflict](/post/high-conflict-summary-by-amanda-ripley) explains the extreme version. Headlee is about the everyday version: the one-to-one where you never actually met the other person because you were both giving speeches.

The fix is unfashionable. Reps. Ordinary conversations treated as practice. Not only the career-defining ones.

**Big Idea 2 — Questions beat opinions**

Open questions. Then follow the answer, not your agenda.

This is [Ask](/post/summary-ask-by-jeff-wetzler). This is [Humble Inquiry](/post/summary-humble-inquiry-by-edgar-schein). Headlee’s contribution is the journalist’s discipline. She cannot fake interest for an hour on live radio. She has to be curious or the interview dies.

Closed questions give you yes/no and a dead end. “Did you like the role?” dies. “How did you come to take it?” opens a story. David Brooks uses the same move. Headlee got there from a different trade.

**Big Idea 3 — Brevity is respect**

I have confused thoroughness with care. A four-minute answer to a thirty-second question is not care. It is occupancy.

Headlee wants you to say the thing, then stop. Let silence work. If they want more, they will ask. This is also Fisher: say less. It is also Scott: let silence do the heavy lifting.

For leaders who present, this is the whole game. [The First Minute](/post/the-first-minute-by-chris-fenning) is the tactical version. Headlee is the conversational version. Frame. Be brief. Ask.

A note on tone. The book repeats the TED talk. If you have watched it twice you will feel the echo. I still wanted the book because I needed the examples around family, race, and talking across difference. Those chapters are why it is not only a listicle bound in hardcover.

> “You can’t learn anything if you’re the one talking.”
