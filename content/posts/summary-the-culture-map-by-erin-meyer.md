## Should You Read This?

**Toby's Rating: 9/10**

[The Culture Map](https://www.amazon.com/dp/1610392507) by Erin Meyer is the most useful communication book I’ve read that isn’t about a difficult conversation. It’s about the water you’re already swimming in. Eight scales: communicating, evaluating, persuading, leading, deciding, trusting, disagreeing, scheduling.

If you lead a team that spans countries — or even two companies after a merger — this will explain fights you’ve been having for years and blaming on personality.

I’d rather you read this than another generic “how to be a better communicator” article.

## Toby's Top Takeaway From The Culture Map by Erin Meyer

I thought I was being clear.

I grew up and worked in Britain. Then I spent years in large organisations that pretended to be global and still ran on Anglo meeting rules. Speak up. Put it in the deck. Disagree in the room. Write the actions in an email.

On Meyer’s scale that’s low-context. The words are supposed to do the work. Say what you mean.

I’ve sat with colleagues for whom that style isn’t honest. It’s blunt. The real message lives in what isn’t said, who is in the room, and what happened last quarter. High-context. If you need it in writing, you’ve already failed the relationship.

I used to call the silence “a lack of challenge.” Sometimes it was fear. Sometimes it was a no they expected me to hear.

The evaluating scale is the one that’s saved me from the most damage.

In the Netherlands, direct negative feedback can be a form of respect. In Japan, or much of South-East Asia, the same sentence in a group setting can be humiliating. I’ve given “helpful” feedback on a project call and watched a face close. I thought I was being [Radically Candid](/post/summary-radical-candor-by-kim-scott). I was being culturally illiterate.

Kim Scott is still right. Care personally, challenge directly. Meyer adds the missing question: **direct compared to whom?**

The move I now make before a cross-border steering group:

1. Who is in the room, and where do they sit on the eight scales relative to me?
2. Will disagreement be heard as loyalty or as attack?
3. Should the hard feedback be in the room, or in a private one-to-one first?
4. Do we decide in the meeting, or is the meeting theatre and the decision happens later?

If you skip that, you’ll run a “healthy debate” that only the loudest culture enjoys. Then you’ll wonder why the work stalls after the call.

Culture is often a design problem wearing a people-problem costume. I’ve written that line before. Meyer gives you the diagram.

## 3 Big Ideas From The Culture Map by Erin Meyer

**1. The gap between you and them matters more than the score**

Meyer’s maps are relative. The UK looks direct next to Japan and soft next to Germany. An American manager in Paris is not “the American style.” They’re more low-context than their French colleagues on some scales and less confrontational on others.

Leaders get this wrong. They learn a stereotype — “Germans are blunt” — and then miss the person in front of them. Use the scale as a hypothesis. Then ask how feedback is usually given here. Ask when a meeting is a decision and when it is a ceremony.

**2. Trust is not one thing**

Task-based trust: I trust you because you delivered the file. The relationship can start in a meeting.

Relationship-based trust: I trust you because we’ve eaten, waited, and survived something. Harder to enter. Then loyal.

I’ve tried to “get to the point” with people who were still deciding whether I was safe. I looked efficient. I was expensive. The later disagreement cost more than the lunch I skipped.

If you’re installing AI or a new way of working across regions, this scale will eat you. The content of the change is the easy part. The trust path is the work.

**3. Disagreement and decision-making are cultural, not moral**

Some cultures disagree in public and still like each other at dinner. Some smile in the room and then the no arrives through a back channel. Neither is more adult.

Consensual cultures take longer up front and move faster after. Top-down cultures decide quicker and then spend months on “buy-in.” I’ve watched British and American leaders interpret consensus as weakness. I’ve watched the reverse: a delayed yes that was never a yes.

Name the rule before the programme starts. How will we disagree? Who decides? When is it locked?

Scheduling is the unglamorous eighth scale and it ruins weeks. If half the team treats a 10am start as a suggestion, your “accountability” speech is a culture speech. Say so.
