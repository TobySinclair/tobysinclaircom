## Should You Read This?

**Toby's Rating: 9/10**

[The Culture Map](https://www.amazon.com/dp/1610392507) by Erin Meyer is the most useful communication book I have read that is not about a difficult conversation. It is about the water you are already swimming in. Eight scales. Communicating, evaluating, persuading, leading, deciding, trusting, disagreeing, scheduling.

If you lead a team that spans countries — or even two companies after a merger — this will explain fights you have been having for years and blaming on personality.

I would rather you read this than another generic “how to be a better communicator” listicle.

## Toby's Top Takeaway From The Culture Map by Erin Meyer

I thought I was being clear.

I grew up and worked in Britain. Then I spent years in large organisations that pretended to be global and still ran on Anglo meeting rules. Speak up. Put it in the deck. Disagree in the room. Write the actions in an email that a lawyer could read.

On Meyer’s communicating scale that is low-context. The words are supposed to do the work. Say what you mean.

I have sat with colleagues for whom that style is not honest. It is blunt. The real message lives in what is not said, who is in the room, and what happened last quarter. High-context. If you need it in writing, you have already failed the relationship.

I used to call the silence “a lack of challenge.” Sometimes it was fear. Sometimes it was a no they expected me to hear.

That is the bruise this book leaves. Your good communication is someone else’s violence. Their good manners are your fog.

The evaluating scale is the one that has saved me from the most damage.

In the Netherlands or Israel, direct negative feedback can be a form of respect. In Japan, or much of South-East Asia, the same sentence in a group setting can be humiliating. I have given “helpful” feedback in a project call and watched a face close. I thought I was being [Radically Candid](/post/summary-radical-candor-by-kim-scott). I was being culturally illiterate.

Kim Scott is still right. Care personally, challenge directly. Meyer adds the missing question: **direct compared to whom?**

The move I now make before a cross-border steering group:

1. Who is in the room, and where do they sit on the eight scales relative to me?
2. Will disagreement be heard as loyalty or as attack?
3. Should the hard feedback be in the room, or in a private one-to-one first?
4. Do we decide in the meeting, or is the meeting theatre and the decision happens later?

If you skip that, you will run a “healthy debate” that only the loudest culture enjoys. Then you will wonder why the work stalls after the call.

This is not etiquette. It is operating system design. Culture is often a design problem wearing a people-problem costume. I have written that line before. Meyer gives you the diagram.

## 3 Big Ideas From The Culture Map by Erin Meyer

**Big Idea 1 — The gap between you and them matters more than the absolute score**

Meyer’s maps are relative. The UK looks direct next to Japan and soft next to Germany. An American manager in Paris is not “the American style.” They are more low-context than their French colleagues on some scales and less confrontational on others.

Leaders get this wrong. They learn a stereotype — “Germans are blunt” — and then miss the person in front of them. Use the scale as a hypothesis. Then do [Humble Inquiry](/post/summary-humble-inquiry-by-edgar-schein). Ask how feedback is usually given here. Ask when a meeting is a decision and when it is a ceremony.

I treat the eight scales as a pre-mortem for the conversation. Same habit as preparing a [crucial conversation](/post/summary-crucial-conversations-by-kerry-patterson). Different variables.

**Big Idea 2 — Trust is not one thing**

Task-based trust: I trust you because you delivered the file. Peach cultures. Soft outside, the relationship can start in a meeting.

Relationship-based trust: I trust you because we have eaten, waited, and survived something. Coconut cultures. Hard to enter, then loyal.

I have tried to “get to the point” with people who were still deciding whether I was safe. I looked efficient. I was expensive. The later disagreement cost more than the lunch I skipped.

If you are installing AI, a new operating model, or a culture programme across regions, this scale will eat you. The content of the change is the easy part. The trust path is the work. That is why I keep pointing enterprises at the human side, not the tool.

**Big Idea 3 — Disagreement and decision-making are cultural, not moral**

Some cultures disagree in public and still like each other at dinner. Some smile in the room and then the no arrives through a back channel. Neither is more adult.

Consensual decision cultures (think many Japanese and Swedish rooms) take longer up front and move faster after. Top-down cultures decide quicker and then spend months on “buy-in.” I have watched British and American leaders interpret consensus as weakness. I have watched the reverse: a delayed yes that was never a yes.

Name the rule before the programme starts. How will we disagree? Who decides? When is it locked? Put it in writing. That is [Getting to Yes](/post/summary-getting-to-yes-by-roger-fisher-william-ury) applied to culture: objective criteria, not vibes.

Scheduling is the unglamorous eighth scale and it ruins weeks. Linear time versus flexible time. If half the team treats a 10am start as a suggestion, your “accountability” speech is a culture speech. Say so.

## Best Quotes From The Culture Map by Erin Meyer

> “The ways we are culturally different or similar can play a powerful role in determining how we think, work, and communicate.”

> “Leaders have always needed to understand human nature and personality differences to be successful. But when you add culture to the mix, the complexity is greater than ever.”

> “When interacting with someone from another culture, try to watch more, listen more, and speak less.”

> “People from low-context cultures put their thoughts into words. People from high-context cultures often leave the most important part unsaid.”

> “The point is not to copy the other culture. The point is to understand the gap and manage it.”
