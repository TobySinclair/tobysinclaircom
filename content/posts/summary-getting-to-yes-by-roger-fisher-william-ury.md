## Should You Read This?

**Toby's Rating: 8/10**

[Getting to Yes](https://www.amazon.com/dp/0143118757) is the book behind half the conversation books on this site. Harvard Negotiation Project. Five million copies. [Difficult Conversations](/post/summary-difficult-conversations) is the sequel in spirit. [Never Split the Difference](/post/never-split-the-difference-summary) is the flashier field manual.

Read this first if you still bargain over positions: my date versus your date, my scope versus your scope, my budget versus yours. Fisher, Ury and Patton will feel a little dated. The method is not.

## Toby's Top Takeaway From Getting to Yes by Roger Fisher, William Ury, and Bruce Patton

I used to think negotiation was a personality.

Some people enjoy the haggle. I do not. I want the meeting to end. So I either fold too early or I dig into a position because folding felt weak.

Both are positional bargaining. Soft. Hard. Same game.

Getting to Yes names the alternative: principled negotiation.

The line I still use in delivery meetings:

**Be hard on the problem, soft on the people.**

I have sat in rooms where we reversed it. Soft on the problem — the date slipped again, nobody said it. Hard on the people — tone, blame, the quiet exile of whoever owned the slide.

The practical shift is from positions to interests.

Position: “We need it by the 30th.”
Interest: “We have a board paper on the 2nd and I cannot walk in empty.”

Once the interest is on the table, the 30th is only one option. A draft by the 27th might do. A smaller release might do. A joint paper might do.

I learned this late. For years I argued dates as if the date were the need. The need was credibility. Or rest. Or not looking incompetent in front of a sponsor.

Chris Voss would tell you to label the emotion and ask calibrated questions. He is right. I rated [Never Split the Difference](/post/never-split-the-difference-summary) a 10 because it is usable under adrenaline. Getting to Yes is the skeleton that book is hanging on. If you only read Voss you can sound clever and still miss the interest.

The other idea I take into every sticky conversation is BATNA. Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement.

What will I do if this meeting produces nothing?

If I do not know, I negotiate scared. I accept fuzzy yeses. I walk out “aligned” and nothing is written down. [Crucial Conversations](/post/summary-crucial-conversations-by-kerry-patterson) would call that failing to move to action. Same failure. Different decade.

Write the BATNA before you walk in. Not as a threat. As a spine.

## 3 Big Ideas From Getting to Yes by Roger Fisher, William Ury, and Bruce Patton

**Big Idea 1 — Separate the people from the problem**

The person across the table is not the deadline. They are a human with a face to save, a boss to please, and a story about you.

If you attack them, they defend their face. The problem sits there, untouched.

Fisher and Ury want two conversations at once. One about the working relationship. One about the substance. Mix them and both go badly. This is the ancestor of the three conversations in [Difficult Conversations](/post/summary-difficult-conversations): what happened, feelings, identity.

In practice I name the relationship first. “I want us to be able to work this without it getting personal.” Then I put the problem on the wall. Whiteboard. Shared doc. Anywhere that is not their chest.

**Big Idea 2 — Invent options before you choose one**

Positional bargaining offers two options. Mine. Yours. Split the difference. Voss is right that splitting the difference can be lazy. The Harvard crowd’s answer is not “never split.” It is “invent first.”

I still run this badly when I am tired. I arrive with one solution and I sell it. That is telling. [Humble Inquiry](/post/summary-humble-inquiry-by-edgar-schein) would wince.

Better: ten minutes of ugly options with no commitment. A later date. A smaller scope. More people. Fewer features. A pilot. A written risk. Then — and only then — apply a standard.

Which brings us to the fourth principle. Insist on objective criteria. Market rate. Precedent. A regulatory date. A definition of done. Something that is not “because I said so.” Status arguments drain teams. Criteria arguments can still be fierce. They do not have to be personal.

**Big Idea 3 — Know your BATNA, and theirs**

Power in a negotiation is not volume. It is the quality of your walk-away.

If your alternative is terrible, you will cling. If their alternative is excellent, your clever framing will not save you. So map both.

I have watched senior rooms skip this and then act shocked when the vendor will not move. The vendor had a better BATNA. We had hope.

BATNA is also how you stay decent. You do not need to bully when you have a real alternative. You can be curious. You can ask what they need. You can use [Nonviolent Communication](/post/summary-nonviolent-communication-by-marshall-rosenberg) without becoming a doormat.

A note on tone. Getting to Yes is calm to the point of being polite. Real negotiations are not. Pair it with a book that admits the heat — [High Conflict](/post/high-conflict-summary-by-amanda-ripley) or Fisher’s [Next Conversation](/post/summary-the-next-conversation-by-jefferson-fisher). Method plus nervous system. That is the set.
