I build AI roleplay simulations for a living at [Real Talk Studio](https://www.realtalkstudio.com). Which means I've watched more people practise their first hard conversation as a manager than almost anyone.

Here's what I've noticed. Nobody fails on the theory.

They've read the books. They know the feedback models. They can tell you what [radical candor](/post/summary-radical-candor-by-kim-scott) means.

Then they sit down opposite a team member who used to be their peer, open their mouth, and spend two minutes warming up with small talk the other person can smell. The actual sentence, the one the meeting exists for, arrives late, softened, and wrapped in so much padding it barely lands.

That's the gap most new manager training never touches. Courses teach models. The job is conversations.

Julie Zhuo's [Making of a Manager](/post/book-summary-the-making-of-a-manager-by-julie-zhuo) is still the best book on the role. [The Coaching Habit](/post/book-summary-coaching-habit-by-michael-bungay-stanier) is the best set of questions. Neither one can sit in the silence after you've said the hard sentence. That part only arrives with practice.

## What new manager training usually covers (and what it misses)

A typical first-time manager programme covers delegation, prioritisation, feedback frameworks, maybe a personality assessment. All useful. None of it is the thing that keeps new managers awake.

What keeps them awake is Tuesday at 2pm, when they have to tell someone something that person doesn't want to hear.

The evidence for this is sitting in my transcripts. When people practise on our platform, they don't choose "how to run a prioritisation exercise". They choose the conversations they're dreading. The former peer. The underperformer. The first team meeting after bad news.

So instead of another framework, here are the conversations I'd prepare every new manager for, and what I've learned watching people rehearse each one. (If you want a longer list, I also published [ten difficult conversation scenarios for managers](/post/difficult-conversation-scenarios-managers).)

## The five conversations that define your first year

### 1. The first team meeting

Your first meeting as the team's manager sets the temperature for everything after it. And the mistake I see constantly in practice sessions is the same one: new managers try to arrive with answers.

You don't have answers yet. Everyone in the room knows it. Pretending otherwise costs you credibility on day one.

What works instead is stating uncertainty plainly and pairing it with something concrete. "I don't know yet how we'll change the roadmap. Here's what I do know, and here's when you'll hear more from me." In our simulations, the managers who say some version of that sentence keep the room. The ones who bluff lose it within minutes, because a room full of people asks questions, and bluffing survives about two of them.

### 2. Managing a former peer

Last week you were complaining about leadership together at the pub. This week you're leadership.

The failure mode here isn't harshness. It's the opposite. New managers over-correct into friendliness, avoid every hard call for months, and then have to have a much worse conversation later, with someone who now feels ambushed because "you never said anything before".

The conversation to have is the early, small, honest one. Name the change. "This is a bit strange for both of us. Some things between us will change, and I'd rather we talked about that than pretended it hasn't happened." It's an awkward ninety seconds that prevents an awful ninety days.

### 3. The first piece of hard feedback

Here's the pattern from the practice transcripts, and it's remarkably consistent. People giving difficult feedback do three things:

- They open with praise that isn't the point, so the other person braces for the "but"
- They deliver the actual message in the passive voice, so nobody owns it
- They rush to reassurance before the message has landed

The fix isn't a cleverer framework. It's saying the sentence. "The report you sent the client had errors in it, and it's the second time." Full stop. Then silence. The silence is the hard part, and it's the part you can't learn from a book, because books can't be silent at you.

### 4. Saying no upwards

Nobody warns new managers that their hardest conversations often point up, not down.

One of our most-requested scenarios is a director asking a team lead to absorb "one more thing", a three-week workstream, presented as a small favour on the way out of the door. The character is built to treat anything short of a clear no as a yes. Because that's what happens in real life.

What I've watched, over and over: people think they've said no when they've actually said "I'll see what I can do". Practise hearing yourself say the actual word. "No, I can't take that on without dropping something. Which would you like me to drop?" It's uncomfortable exactly once per relationship. Then it becomes how you operate.

### 5. Delivering bad news you didn't choose

Redundancies, restructures, a cancelled project. As a new manager you'll deliver decisions you didn't make and might not agree with.

We built a team simulation of exactly this: the first team meeting an hour after a redundancy email goes out. Four AI team members react the way real rooms do. One is scared and wants numbers you don't have. One is angry and wants someone to blame. One has checked out entirely. One insists everything's fine, which somehow makes it worse.

The lesson from watching people run that room: you can't fix the emotions, and trying to makes you sound like a press release. What you can do is acknowledge what's true, hold the line on what you don't know, and give the next concrete step. Managers who do those three things get through it with their team's trust intact. Managers who try to spin it don't.

If this is the conversation you're actually facing, I wrote a separate [practical, humane guide to the redundancy conversation](/post/redundancy-conversation-guide) — the sentence, the pause, and the meeting with everyone else afterwards.

## Why practice beats theory (and why nobody practises)

If conversations are the job, the obvious question is why new manager training involves so little practising of them.

The honest answer is that practising with humans is awkward. Role play in a training room means performing in front of colleagues, being judged, and playing against a facilitator who's far too polite to give you a realistic hard time. Most people would rather skip it. So most people do, and their first attempt at the redundancy meeting is the real one.

This is the problem I started Real Talk Studio to solve. [AI roleplay](/post/ai-roleplay-training) gives you the reps without the audience: a realistic counterpart who pushes back, gets emotional, interrupts, and doesn't perform politeness. You can be bad at the conversation in private, which is the only place anyone is willing to be bad at anything.

But whatever tool you use, the principle is the one that matters. Pilots don't read about crosswinds and then land in one. Your first difficult conversation as a manager shouldn't be a live one.

## How to build your own practice routine

If you're a new manager, or you run training for them:

- List the five conversations above and rank them by dread. Start with the top one.
- Write the single sentence each conversation exists to deliver. If you can't write it, you're not ready to say it.
- Rehearse out loud, not in your head. Your head is a flattering audience.
- Practise against resistance. A colleague playing devil's advocate, or [an AI simulation that pushes back properly](https://www.realtalkstudio.com).
- Debrief on one thing only: did you say the sentence, early and plainly?
