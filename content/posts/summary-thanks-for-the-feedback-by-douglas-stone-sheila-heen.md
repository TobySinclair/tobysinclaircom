## Should You Read This?

**Toby's Rating: 9/10**

[Thanks for the Feedback](https://www.amazon.com/dp/0143127136) by Douglas Stone and Sheila Heen is the book I should have read before I started collecting books about giving feedback. [Radical Candor](/post/summary-radical-candor-by-kim-scott). [Crucial Conversations](/post/summary-crucial-conversations-by-kerry-patterson). [Fierce Conversations](/post/summary-fierce-conversations-by-susan-scott). All of them assume the other person can take it.

Most can’t. I often can’t.

This is the companion to [Difficult Conversations](/post/summary-difficult-conversations). If that book taught you how to say the thing, this one teaches you how to hear the thing.

## Toby's Top Takeaway From Thanks for the Feedback by Douglas Stone and Sheila Heen

I asked for feedback. Then I defended myself for twelve minutes.

Classic.

They said the session ran long. I explained the agenda. They said people looked lost. I explained the audience. They said I interrupted. I explained that I was adding value.

I wasn’t receiving feedback. I was prosecuting a case against it.

Stone and Heen have a name for the three wires that trip us:

- **Truth triggers.** “That’s wrong.” I argue the facts.
- **Relationship triggers.** “Who are you to tell me?” I argue the person.
- **Identity triggers.** “If this is true, what does that say about me?” I argue for my life.

I hit identity fastest. Coaching is supposed to be my craft. Feedback that I talked too much lands as “you’re a fraud.” So I smile, and then I explain.

The most useful idea in the book is simple. There are three types of feedback and we mash them together.

1. **Appreciation.** Thanks. I see you. Keep going.
2. **Coaching.** Here’s a better way.
3. **Evaluation.** Here’s where you stand.

I give coaching when people are starving for appreciation. I’ve done this in one-to-ones for years. Someone finishes a brutal quarter. I jump to “next time, tighten the narrative.” They hear: you are not enough. They needed “I saw what that took.”

I also hide evaluation inside coaching. “A few thoughts for next time” when I’ve already decided they’re not ready. They can feel the grade under the advice.

The shift the book asks for is the one I still resist: you are the one who can make feedback useful. The giver will be clumsy. Waiting for a perfect giver is how leaders stop learning.

A question I now ask before I reply:

**What type is this, and which trigger just went off?**

If I can name those two things, I can stay in the conversation. If I can’t, I’ll win the next twelve minutes and lose the information.

## 3 Big Ideas From Thanks for the Feedback by Douglas Stone and Sheila Heen

**1. Separate appreciation, coaching, and evaluation**

Before you respond, label the type. Before you give, say the type out loud.

“I want to give you appreciation, not coaching.”
“This is evaluation. Then we can coach.”
“I asked for coaching. I’m not looking for a rating today.”

Teams that don’t name the type live in fog. High performers burn out because they only get evaluation. Struggling people get appreciation when they needed a standard. Everyone says they want more feedback. They usually want a different type than the one you’re offering.

In a one-to-one I now ask: do you want to be thanked, coached, or told where you stand?

**2. The trigger is information**

A truth trigger might mean the feedback is wrong. It might also mean you have a story that can’t survive contact with their data. You won’t know which until you get curious.

A relationship trigger is often about history, not the content. “I can’t hear this from you” is a real sentence. Name it. Then decide whether you can still use the data.

An identity trigger is the loudest. Feedback collapses into “I am good” or “I am finished.” Stone and Heen want a more adult version: I can be skilled and unfinished.

I still feel the heat. I just try not to talk in the first thirty seconds.

**3. Go and get the coaching**

Waiting to be managed is a bad strategy.

- Ask for one thing. “What’s one thing I could do differently in the steering meeting?”
- Ask for an example. “When did you see it?”
- Ask for the movie, not the label. “What did I do?” not “Am I strategic enough?”

If your culture only trains managers to give feedback, you’ve built a broadcasting system. Receiving is the skill that turns the broadcast into change.

## Best Quotes From Thanks for the Feedback by Douglas Stone and Sheila Heen

> “Appreciation is about relationships. Coaching is about learning. Evaluation is about ranking, rating, and deciding.”

> “It’s not the quality of the advice that determines whether feedback is useful. It’s whether you can use it.”

> “We can’t control the skill of the person giving us feedback. We can control how we receive it.”
