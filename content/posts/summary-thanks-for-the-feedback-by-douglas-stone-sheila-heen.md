## Should You Read This?

**Toby's Rating: 9/10**

[Thanks for the Feedback](https://www.amazon.com/dp/0143127136) by Douglas Stone and Sheila Heen is the book I should have read before I started collecting books about giving feedback. [Radical Candor](/post/summary-radical-candor-by-kim-scott). [Crucial Conversations](/post/summary-crucial-conversations-by-kerry-patterson). [Fierce Conversations](/post/summary-fierce-conversations-by-susan-scott). All of them assume the other person can take it.

Most cannot. I often cannot.

This is the companion to [Difficult Conversations](/post/summary-difficult-conversations) from the same Harvard Negotiation Project stable. If that book taught you how to say the thing, this one teaches you how to hear the thing without making it about your identity.

ISBN: 9780143127130

## Toby's Top Takeaway From Thanks for the Feedback by Douglas Stone and Sheila Heen

I asked for feedback. Then I defended myself for twelve minutes.

Classic.

They said the session ran long. I explained the agenda. They said people looked lost. I explained the audience. They said I interrupted. I explained that I was adding value.

I was not receiving feedback. I was prosecuting a case against it.

Stone and Heen have a name for the three wires that trip us:

- **Truth triggers.** “That’s wrong.” I argue the facts.
- **Relationship triggers.** “Who are you to tell me?” I argue the person.
- **Identity triggers.** “If this is true, what does that say about me?” I argue for my life.

I hit identity fastest. Coaching is supposed to be my craft. Feedback that I talked too much lands as “you are a fraud.” So I smile, and then I explain.

The most useful idea in the book is embarrassingly simple. There are three types of feedback and we mash them together.

1. **Appreciation.** Thanks. I see you. Keep going.
2. **Coaching.** Here’s a better way.
3. **Evaluation.** Here’s where you stand. Rating, ranking, the decision.

I give coaching when people are starving for appreciation. I have done this in one-to-ones for years. Someone finishes a brutal quarter. I jump to “next time, tighten the narrative.” They hear: you are not enough. They needed “I saw what that took.”

I also hide evaluation inside coaching. “A few thoughts for next time” when I have already decided they are not ready. That is cowardice. They can feel the grade under the advice.

The shift the book asks for is the one I still resist: you are the one who can make feedback useful. The giver will be clumsy. They will mix types. They will be late and vague and a bit political. Waiting for a perfect giver is how leaders stop learning.

A question I now ask before I reply:

**What type is this, and which trigger just went off?**

If I can name those two things, I can stay in the conversation. If I cannot, I will win the next twelve minutes and lose the information.

This is why I built [Real Talk Studio](https://www.realtalkstudio.com/). Not only to practise the giving. To practise the receiving. The second one is where careers stall.

## 3 Big Ideas From Thanks for the Feedback by Douglas Stone and Sheila Heen

**Big Idea 1 — Separate appreciation, coaching, and evaluation**

Before you respond, label the type. Before you give, say the type out loud.

“I want to give you appreciation, not coaching.”
“This is evaluation. Then we can coach.”
“I asked for coaching. I’m not looking for a rating today.”

Teams that do not name the type live in fog. High performers burn out because they only get evaluation. Struggling people get appreciation when they needed a standard. Everyone says they want more feedback. They usually want a different type than the one you are offering.

In a one-to-one I now ask: do you want to be thanked, coached, or told where you stand? It is a cousin of Duhigg’s “helped, hugged, or heard” in [Supercommunicators](/post/summary-supercommunicators-by-charles-duhigg). Same instinct. Clearer labels.

**Big Idea 2 — The trigger is information**

A truth trigger might mean the feedback is wrong. It might also mean you have a story that cannot survive contact with their data. You will not know which until you get curious.

A relationship trigger is often about history, status, or trust — not the content. “I can’t hear this from you” is a real sentence. Name it. Then decide whether you can still use the data.

An identity trigger is the loudest. Feedback collapses into “I am good” or “I am finished.” Stone and Heen want a more adult identity: I am someone who can be both skilled and unfinished. That is the only identity that can learn.

I still feel the heat. I just try not to talk in the first thirty seconds. [Jefferson Fisher](/post/summary-the-next-conversation-by-jefferson-fisher) would call that the breath. Same move. Different bookshelf.

**Big Idea 3 — Pull coaching toward you**

Waiting to be managed is a bad strategy. The book’s best leaders go and get it.

- Ask for one thing. “What’s one thing I could do differently in the steering meeting?”
- Ask for an example. “When did you see it?”
- Ask for the movie, not the label. “What did I do?” not “Am I strategic enough?”
- Separate the person from the data. You can reject the tone and still keep the observation.

This is feedforward as well as feedback. [Marshall Goldsmith](/post/summary-what-got-you-here-wont-get-you-there-by-marshall-goldsmith) would recognise it. Ask what to do next time, not for a verdict on last year.

The organisation implication is the one I care about. If your culture only trains managers to give feedback, you have built a broadcasting system. Receiving is the skill that turns the broadcast into change. Otherwise you get the Corporate Nod from [Fierce Conversations](/post/summary-fierce-conversations-by-susan-scott) and nothing moves.
