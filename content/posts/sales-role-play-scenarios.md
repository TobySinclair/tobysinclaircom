Ask a sales team about role play and watch the room flinch.

Everyone's been in that team meeting. The manager says "let's do a quick role play", two reps shuffle to the front, and what follows is theatre. The rep performs for their manager, not for a customer. The colleague playing the buyer is far too polite to object properly. Everyone claps. Nobody learned anything, and the actual objection that kills deals goes unpractised for another quarter.

I build sales roleplay simulations for a living at [Real Talk Studio](https://www.realtalkstudio.com), including cold-call and negotiation scenarios for contact-centre and key-account teams. So I've thought a lot about why traditional sales role play fails, and I've watched what happens when you fix it. This article covers both, plus five scenarios you can run this week.

If you want the negotiation books behind the drills, start with [Never Split the Difference](/post/never-split-the-difference-summary) and [Pitch Anything](/post/summary-pitch-anything-by-oren-klaff). Those teach the moves. These scenarios are where you find out whether you can still use them when someone is trying to hang up.

## Why most sales role play doesn't work

Three design flaws, and they compound:

**There's an audience.** The moment colleagues are watching, the rep's goal changes from "handle the customer" to "don't look stupid". Those are different skills. Performance anxiety isn't a small tax on learning; in my experience it replaces the learning entirely.

**The buyer is too nice.** Colleagues playing customers give one soft objection and then buy. Real prospects say "I'm busy", "I'm happy with my current supplier", and "just email me something", usually in the first twenty seconds, usually in a clipped tone that offers you nothing to work with. If the practice buyer doesn't do that, you're rehearsing a fiction.

**There are no reps in the reps.** One role play per quarter is a demonstration, not training. Skill comes from volume: the same scenario, attempted repeatedly, with feedback between attempts. No sales team runs role play at that frequency with humans, because humans have day jobs and finite patience.

The common thread: traditional role play optimises for the comfort of everyone except the customer who isn't in the room.

## What a good sales role play scenario needs

When we design a scenario, whether for a cold-calling floor or a key account manager defending price, it has five ingredients:

- **A counterpart with a life.** The buyer has a reason to be difficult. They're mid-task, they've been burned before, they have a boss of their own.
- **Stock objections, front-loaded.** The realistic brush-offs arrive early, because that's when they arrive in reality. The opening window of a cold call, roughly the first twenty to forty seconds, is where the call is won or lost.
- **One clear number or decision.** Tight scope beats sprawling improv. One price floor, one threat, one trade to find.
- **No audience.** Practice should be private. People are only willing to be bad at something in private, and being bad at it is the first stage of getting good.
- **Feedback on what was actually said.** Not "good energy!", but "you conceded 5% ninety seconds in, and the buyer hadn't asked for it yet".

Hold your current role play practice against those five. Most team-meeting versions score zero.

## Five sales role play scenarios to run this week

Steal these. Each includes the setup, the counterpart's brief, and what to watch for.

### 1. The cold open

**Setup.** Outbound call to a prospect from an old comparison-site lead. They half-remember filling in a form two years ago. They're busy.

**Counterpart's brief.** Short, clipped replies. Lead with stock objections: "I'm busy", "I'm happy with my supplier", "just email me". Warm up only if the rep earns it.

**Watch for.** Whether the rep sidesteps objections or argues with them. Arguing with "I'm busy" is a hang-up; acknowledging it and asking for thirty seconds sometimes isn't. The whole game is the first half-minute.

### 2. Holding the line on price

**Setup.** A buyer with a three-year relationship demands 8% off for a promotion and mentions a range review, which both of you understand as a delist threat. The rep's floor is 3%.

**Counterpart's brief.** Friendly but immovable on wanting a number. Uses the relationship as leverage. Escalates the threat if the rep stonewalls without offering anything.

**Watch for.** The panic concession. Most reps give ground inside the first minute just to make the tension stop. The skill is holding the number while trading structure: display space, volume commitments, promo length.

### 3. The stalled deal

**Setup.** A deal that was "definitely closing this month" two months ago. The champion has gone quiet. The rep has one call to find out what's real.

**Counterpart's brief.** Evasive, apologetic, hiding the actual blocker (budget got pulled, or a rival got in). Reveals the truth only if asked a direct question.

**Watch for.** Whether the rep asks the scary question. "Is this deal still alive?" feels dangerous to say, which is exactly why it goes unsaid, and why dead deals haunt pipelines for quarters.

### 4. The objection gauntlet

**Setup.** Not a full call, a drill. The buyer fires the rep's five most common objections, one after another, no small talk between them.

**Counterpart's brief.** Rapid fire. Accept nothing that isn't a direct, structured response. Repeat any objection that gets a waffled answer.

**Watch for.** Consistency. Anyone can handle one objection with a run-up. The fifth in ninety seconds shows you what's actually automatic.

### 5. The renewal that isn't safe

**Setup.** An account the rep considers locked in. The buyer opens with: "Before we renew, I should tell you we've been talking to your competitor."

**Counterpart's brief.** Not bluffing. Has a genuinely better offer in hand, but also real switching costs they'll admit to if the conversation goes well.

**Watch for.** Complacency shattering into either panic-discounting or defensiveness. The strong path is curiosity: "That's fair, tell me what they're offering that we're not."

## Making practice a habit, not an event

The scenarios matter less than the frequency. A rep who runs the cold open ten times this month will outgrow a rep who does one workshop this year, whatever the workshop costs.

You can build that frequency with peer practice if your team has the discipline and the honesty for it. The brief matters: instruct whoever plays the buyer to be realistically difficult, and protect the rep from an audience.
