## Should You Read This?

**Toby's Rating: 9/10**

[The Next Conversation](https://www.amazon.com/dp/0593718724) by Jefferson Fisher is the most practical hard-conversation book I have read since [Fierce Conversations](/post/summary-fierce-conversations-by-susan-scott). Fisher is a Texas trial lawyer who started filming communication tips from his truck. The book is the opposite of a theory dump. Scripts. Pauses. Exact words. I use three of them already.

If you liked [Crucial Conversations](/post/summary-crucial-conversations-by-kerry-patterson) but wanted someone to tell you what to say in the next thirty seconds, start here.

ISBN: 9780593718728

## Toby's Top Takeaway From The Next Conversation by Jefferson Fisher

I still try to win.

Not in a cartoon way. I do not slam the table. I do the sophisticated version. I add one more point. I tidy their logic. I wait for the pause and then I land the line I rehearsed in the car.

The room goes quiet. I feel briefly clever. Then the relationship is a degree colder.

Fisher’s first chapter is a slap: never win an argument.

> Winning an argument is a losing game. When you win, you’ve likely lost something more valuable — their trust, respect, or connection.

I have paid that tax. In my first management role I sent a harsh review of a test plan. I was “right.” The other person started crying. I had a relationship to rebuild. [Kim Scott](/post/summary-radical-candor-by-kim-scott) later named that style for me: obnoxious aggression. Fisher names the habit underneath it. I was playing to win.

The reframe I have been using since I finished the book:

**Have something to learn, not something to prove.**

That is the same stance I try to take as a coach. It is much harder when I am the leader in the room and the work is late.

Fisher’s most useful move is embarrassingly small. Your first word is your breath.

Inhale through the nose. A second short inhale. A long exhale. Then speak.

I talk faster when the stakes rise. I stack issues. I fill silence because silence feels like I am losing. Fisher treats silence as a tool. Short pauses (a second or two) put weight on a sentence. Long pauses (five to ten seconds) hold a mirror up to a rude comment. The other person often edits themselves. I have watched this work in a one-to-one. It is uncomfortable. That is the point.

The other line I have written on a sticky note:

**I need…**

Not “I was just wondering if maybe we could…” Not “Sorry to bother you.” Need is a clean word. It is not aggressive. It is specific. Fisher would also delete the word “just.” I still use it. I am trying to stop.

If you only steal one thing from [The Next Conversation](https://www.amazon.com/dp/0593718724), steal the frame at the start:

1. Set the direction. “I want to talk about yesterday’s client call.”
2. Call your shot. “I want us to leave with a clearer way of handling the next one.”
3. Get their yes. “Does that work?”

One topic. One conversation. Then stop.

Your company still grows or stagnates one conversation at a time. Fisher’s point is sharper: the conversation that repairs the last one matters more than the first impression. That is why the book is called The Next Conversation.

## 3 Big Ideas From The Next Conversation by Jefferson Fisher

**Big Idea 1 — Control yourself before you try to control the room**

Fisher splits an argument into two phases: ignition and cooling. In ignition your body is already in a fight. Stress hormones are up. Clear thinking is down. Most of us talk in that state and then spend a week cleaning up the email.

His sequence is physical first:

- Breath before words.
- A quick body scan. Name the emotion. “I can tell I’m getting defensive.”
- A short internal line that matches the goal. Mine is: seek to understand.

This is not mindfulness theatre. It is what stops you from matching their volume. When they get louder, you get slower. When they insult you, you pause long enough that their sentence has to sit in the room with them.

I used to think composure was a personality trait. Fisher treats it as a technique.

**Big Idea 2 — Confidence is a behaviour, not a feeling**

You cannot decide to feel confident and then speak. You speak more cleanly, and the feeling follows.

Fisher’s edits are the kind you can make in an email today:

- Cut “just,” “sort of,” “actually,” “does that make sense?”
- Start with “I need,” not an apology for existing.
- Say less. Over-explaining sounds like you do not believe yourself.
- Replace filler with silence.
- Disagree from a vantage point: “I see this differently.” Not “You’re wrong.”

This is the opposite of the bore bomb I wrote about in [How to Know a Person](/post/summary-how-to-know-a-person-by-david-brooks). The confident person does not comment on everything. They choose the moment.

He is also useful on difficult people. Repeat the insult slowly. Ask “Did you mean for that to sound rude?” Make them say the belittling line again. I have not enjoyed doing this. It works.

**Big Idea 3 — Connection is a frame, not a vibe**

Fisher defines connection as two-factor authentication: you understand them, and they know you understood. Agreement is optional.

That is why he is so fussy about framing. Unframed conversations wander, then get personal, then become three conversations at once. The missed deadline becomes your tone from March becomes the restructure.

For the hard ones he is blunt:

- Book real time. Private. Phones away.
- Drop the pleasantries. “This will be hard to hear” is kinder than three minutes of weather.
- Begin with the end. Lead with the point. Do not build a mystery.

When someone brings a hard conversation to you: welcome them, ask one open question, ask permission before you share, offer experience instead of orders.

Read this next to [Difficult Conversations](/post/summary-difficult-conversations). Stone, Patton and Heen explain the three conversations underneath the fight. Fisher tells you what to do with your mouth in the next sixty seconds. I want both on the desk.
