## Should You Read This?

**Toby's Rating: 9/10**

[What Got You Here Won’t Get You There](https://www.amazon.com/dp/1401301304) by Marshall Goldsmith is not a theory of communication. It’s a list of twenty interpersonal habits that got you promoted and will now get you stuck. Winning too much. Adding too much value. Starting with no, but, however. Failing to listen.

I read it as a conversation book because that’s where every habit shows up. In the one-to-one. In the steering group. In the Slack thread you should have left alone.

## Toby's Top Takeaway From What Got You Here Won't Get You There by Marshall Goldsmith

I add too much value.

Someone brings an idea that is 90% there. I can’t leave the 10% alone. I add a twist. I’m trying to help. They leave a little less proud of the idea, and a little less likely to bring the next one.

Goldsmith says this is one of the most common leader defects. We spent our early careers being rewarded for the better idea. Then the job changed. The job became making other people bigger. The old habit now subtracts.

Winning too much is the sibling. I still feel it. A debate that doesn’t matter. I still want the last word.

The line that should be on every leadership programme:

**We spend a lot of time teaching leaders what to do. We don’t spend enough time teaching them what to stop.**

Stopping is a conversation skill. You stop interrupting. You stop saying “no, but.” You stop making comments you won’t remember and they won’t forget.

I’ve done the ugly version of this list on myself. I won’t publish the score. I’ll tell you the three that keep returning:

1. Adding too much value.
2. Starting with “no, but, however.”
3. Failing to express gratitude in the moment — I think it, I don’t say it.

Feedforward is the practice I actually use. Don’t ask “how did I do?” Ask “what’s one thing I could do better next time?” Future. Specific. Less threat.

Then advertise the change. Goldsmith is shameless about this. Tell people what you’re trying to stop. Ask them to catch you. Follow up. It feels corny. It’s how behaviour becomes visible enough to die.

This is why so many culture programmes fail. They add a value. They don’t name the habit to stop. Winning too much in senior rooms will beat “collaboration” on the poster every day of the week.

## 3 Big Ideas From What Got You Here Won't Get You There by Marshall Goldsmith

**1. The higher you go, the more your problems are behavioural**

Technical skill got you here. At a certain level everyone is smart. What differentiates you is how you make other people feel when you talk.

Goldsmith’s twenty habits are almost all conversational. Winning too much. Adding too much value. Passing judgement. Making destructive comments. Starting with no, but, however. Telling the world how smart we are. Speaking when angry. Withholding information. Failing to give recognition. Claiming credit. Making excuses. Not listening. Failing to express gratitude. Punishing the messenger. An excessive need to be “me.”

That last one is the most British. “That’s just how I am. I’m direct.” Often a permission slip for being careless.

**2. Apologise, advertise, ask, listen, thank, follow up**

Goldsmith’s change process is not a 90-day transformation. It’s a loop.

Apologise for the habit. A clean one.
Advertise what you’re changing so others know what to look for.
Ask for feedforward.
Listen. Don’t explain.
Thank them.
Follow up. Did they notice?

I’ve used a lighter version with leadership teams. Pick one habit. Tell your peers. Review it in the next offsite. The teams that do this get less theatre. The teams that skip it buy another 360 and change nothing.

**3. Hitting the goal does not excuse the wake**

Leaders excuse bad talk because the target was hit. Goldsmith is not anti-goal. He’s anti the collateral.

I’ve hit dates and lost people. I’ve been “clear” and left a mess.

You can still be ambitious and stop winning the argument about the biscuits.

If you want the organisational cousin of this book, read [Multipliers](/post/multipliers-book-summary). Wiseman is about how you use other people’s intelligence. Goldsmith is about the tics that make them hide it.
