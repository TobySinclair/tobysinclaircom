## Should You Read This?

**Toby's Rating: 9/10**

[What Got You Here Won’t Get You There](https://www.amazon.com/dp/1401301304) by Marshall Goldsmith is not a theory of communication. It is a list of twenty interpersonal habits that got you promoted and will now get you stuck. Winning too much. Adding too much value. Starting with no, but, however. Failing to listen. Withholding information. Playing favourites.

I read it as a conversation book because that is where every habit shows up. In the one-to-one. In the steering group. In the Slack thread you should have left alone.

If [Thanks for the Feedback](/post/summary-thanks-for-the-feedback-by-douglas-stone-sheila-heen) is how you receive the mirror, Goldsmith is what the mirror is usually pointing at.

## Toby's Top Takeaway From What Got You Here Won't Get You There by Marshall Goldsmith

I add too much value.

Someone brings an idea that is 90% there. I cannot leave the 10% alone. I add a twist. I am trying to help. They leave a little less proud of the idea, and a little less likely to bring the next one.

Goldsmith says this is one of the most common leader defects. We spent our early careers being rewarded for the better idea. Then the job changed. The job became making other people bigger. The old habit now subtracts.

Winning too much is the sibling. I still feel it. A debate that does not matter. I still want the last word. Fisher would tell me I have already lost. Goldsmith would tell me to let the other person be right on the small things so they can hear me on the large ones.

The line that should be on every leadership programme:

**We spend a lot of time teaching leaders what to do. We do not spend enough time teaching them what to stop.**

Stopping is a conversation skill. You stop interrupting. You stop saying “no, but.” You stop making destructive comments that you will not remember and they will not forget. [Susan Scott](/post/summary-fierce-conversations-by-susan-scott) calls that the emotional wake. Goldsmith gives you the inventory.

I have done the ugly version of this list on myself. I will not publish the score. I will tell you the three that keep returning:

1. Adding too much value.
2. Starting with “no, but, however.”
3. Failing to express gratitude in the moment — I think it, I do not say it.

Feedforward is the practice I actually use. Do not ask “how did I do?” Ask “what’s one thing I could do better next time?” Future. Specific. Less identity threat. Stone and Heen would approve.

Then advertise the change. Goldsmith is shameless about this. Tell people what you are trying to stop. Ask them to catch you. Follow up. It feels corny. It is how behaviour becomes visible enough to die.

This is why so many culture programmes fail. They add a value. They do not name the habit to stop. Winning too much in senior rooms will beat “collaboration” on the poster every day of the week.

## 3 Big Ideas From What Got You Here Won't Get You There by Marshall Goldsmith

**Big Idea 1 — The higher you go, the more your problems are behavioural**

Technical skill got you here. At a certain level everyone is smart. What differentiates you is how you make other people feel when you talk.

Goldsmith’s twenty habits are almost all conversational:

- Winning too much
- Adding too much value
- Passing judgement
- Making destructive comments
- Starting with no, but, however
- Telling the world how smart we are
- Speaking when angry
- Negativity, or “Let me explain why that won’t work”
- Withholding information
- Failing to give recognition
- Claiming credit that we don’t deserve
- Making excuses
- Clinging to the past
- Playing favourites
- Refusing to express regret
- Not listening
- Failing to express gratitude
- Punishing the messenger
- Passing the buck
- An excessive need to be “me”

That last one is the most British. “That’s just how I am. I’m direct.” Often a permission slip for being careless. [Dare to Lead](/post/summary-dare-to-lead-by-brene-brown) would call it armour. I call it a stalled career with a personality brand.

**Big Idea 2 — Apologise, advertise, ask, listen, thank, follow up**

Goldsmith’s change process is not a 90-day transformation. It is a loop.

Apologise for the habit. Not a fake sorry. A clean one.
Advertise what you are changing so others know what to look for.
Ask for feedforward.
Listen. Do not explain.
Thank them.
Follow up monthly. Did they notice?

I have used a lighter version with leadership teams. Pick one habit. Tell your peers. Review it in the next offsite. The teams that do this get less theatre. The teams that skip it buy another 360 and change nothing.

Pair the listening step with [Just Listen](/post/summary-just-listen-by-mark-goulston) if you get defensive. Pair the asking step with [Humble Inquiry](/post/summary-humble-inquiry-by-edgar-schein). Goldsmith is the agenda. Those books are the craft.

**Big Idea 3 — Goal obsession will justify any conversational damage**

Leaders excuse bad talk because the target was hit. Goldsmith is not anti-goal. He is anti the collateral.

I have hit dates and lost people. I have been “clear” and left a wake. Ruinous empathy is my more common failure now — I spare the feeling and blur the standard. Both are habits. Both show up as sentences in a room.

The adult move is to keep the goal and lose the habit that used to serve it. You can still be ambitious and stop winning the argument about the biscuits.

If you want the organisational cousin of this book, read [Multipliers](/post/multipliers-book-summary). Wiseman is about how you use other people’s intelligence. Goldsmith is about the tics that make them hide it.
