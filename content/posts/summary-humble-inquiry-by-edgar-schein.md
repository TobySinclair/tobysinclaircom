## Should You Read This?

**Toby's Rating: 9/10**

[Humble Inquiry](https://amzn.to/35s04B9) by Edgar Schein is a thin book with a thick habit. I have recommended it for years. I first wrote about it in 2015. I still fail it weekly.

Schein’s definition is the one I use when I train leaders:

> Humble Inquiry is the fine art of drawing someone out, of asking questions to which you do not already know the answer, of building a relationship based on curiosity and interest in the other person.

If you only read one asking book, read [The Coaching Habit](/post/book-summary-coaching-habit-by-michael-bungay-stanier). Then read this. Stanier gives you the questions. Schein explains why your culture will punish you for using them.

## Toby's Top Takeaway From Humble Inquiry by Edgar Schein

I was valued for answers.

Software tester. Then the person who could explain the process. Then the agile coach who, if I am honest, did a lot of mentoring dressed as coaching. I have written about that shift before. 2016. Barefoot. ICF. The craft is asking. The old identity is telling.

Schein names the trap with less romance than the coaching industry. We live in a telling culture. Status goes to the person who already knows. Asking feels like incompetence. So leaders perform certainty. Teams perform agreement. The information that would save the project stays in the mouths of the people closest to the work.

I have been that leader. Someone brings a mess. I feel the itch. I add value. I finish their sentence. I leave the meeting slightly taller and they leave slightly smaller.

[Michael Bungay Stanier](/post/the-advice-trap-summary-by-michael-bungay-stanier) calls it the Advice Monster. Schein calls it the failure of humble inquiry. Same disease.

The sentence I now try to earn:

**I do not know, and I need you to help me see it.**

That is not false humility. I have been in the room. I have a view. I put it down for a minute. I ask a question I cannot already answer.

“What do you see that I don’t?”
“What are you not saying yet?”
“What would you do if I were not here?”

In 2015 I wrote that product owners tell and teams tell back, and nobody asks. I still watch it. The HIPPO speaks. The Corporate Nod from [Susan Scott](/post/summary-fierce-conversations-by-susan-scott). We launch on Monday.

Humble inquiry is how you break the nod. It is also how you get bad news while there is still time. Schein spent a career on that: planes, hospitals, companies that punished the junior person for speaking. Psychological safety is the fashionable name. He was writing the behaviour underneath it.

A caution. Fake questions are worse than clean telling. “Don’t you think we should…?” is a demand in a wig. [Jeff Wetzler](/post/summary-ask-by-jeff-wetzler) calls those sneaky questions. If you already know, say the view, then ask what you are missing. That is still inquiry. It is just honest.

## 3 Big Ideas From Humble Inquiry by Edgar Schein

**Big Idea 1 — Telling is a status move**

When I tell, I place myself above. I may be right. I have still changed the relationship. The other person now has to accept, resist, or pretend.

Asking — real asking — temporarily inverts the status. I become dependent on their knowledge. In a hierarchy that feels dangerous. That is why it is rare. That is why it works.

Schein is not anti-telling. Surgeons tell. Pilots tell. Leaders must decide. The error is telling as the default, including in moments that required learning.

My rule of thumb for one-to-ones: if I have spoken first and longest, I was not coaching. I was broadcasting. [Time to Think](/post/book-summary-time-to-think-by-nancy-kline) makes the same point with silence. Schein makes it with questions.

**Big Idea 2 — There are several kinds of inquiry, and only one of them is humble**

Diagnostic inquiry: I ask to confirm my hypothesis. Useful. Also leading.

Confrontational inquiry: I ask to make a point. “Why did you think that was a good idea?”

Process inquiry: I ask about the conversation itself. “Are we even talking about the right problem?”

Humble inquiry: I ask because I do not know, and the relationship matters as much as the answer.

Leaders over-use the first two. They sound sharp in the meeting. They starve the relationship. Then they wonder why nobody brings them the ugly truth.

If you want a modern companion on question quality, pair this with [A More Beautiful Question](/post/a-more-beautiful-question-summary) and [How to Know Everything](/post/summary-how-to-know-everything-by-elke-wiss). Schein is the shortest and the most organisational.

**Big Idea 3 — Culture eats your curiosity unless you design for it**

You cannot humble-inquire your way through a system that rewards speed, certainty, and the appearance of alignment.

If the senior room mocks questions, people will stop asking. If the stand-up is a status report, nobody will say “I am stuck.” If AI adoption is a mandate with no curiosity about what the work actually is, you will get compliance theatre.

Schein’s later work with his son Peter is blunt about this. Humble leadership is not a workshop. It is a way of relating across levels so that information can travel.

That is why this book sits with my hard-conversation pile, not only the coaching pile. The conversation you are not hearing is often a culture problem. [The Culture Map](/post/summary-the-culture-map-by-erin-meyer) explains the national layer. Schein explains the organisational one.

Try this in your next meeting. Ask one question you do not know the answer to. Then shut up long enough that the silence does the work. If that feels like you have lost control, you have found the habit.
