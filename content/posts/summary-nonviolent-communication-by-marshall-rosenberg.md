## Should You Read This?

**Toby's Rating: 8/10**

[Nonviolent Communication](https://www.amazon.com/dp/189200528X) by Marshall Rosenberg is a classic for a reason. Eight million copies. Satya Nadella made it required reading for Microsoft’s senior team. The four-step model — observation, feeling, need, request — is the cleanest language I know for a hard conversation that does not turn into a prosecution.

It can sound workshop-y. People start sentences with “I’m feeling a need for autonomy” and the room winces. Ignore the giraffe. Keep the structure. Use it in a one-to-one tomorrow.

If you already have [Fierce Conversations](/post/summary-fierce-conversations-by-susan-scott) and [Radical Candor](/post/summary-radical-candor-by-kim-scott) on the shelf, this is the missing grammar.

## Toby's Top Takeaway From Nonviolent Communication by Marshall Rosenberg

I am very good at diagnosis.

“They’re not committed.”
“They’re political.”
“They don’t get it.”

Those sentences feel like facts. They are not. They are moral judgements wearing a lanyard.

Rosenberg calls this jackal language. Blame, labels, shoulds, comparisons. It is the default dialect of most leadership meetings I have sat in. Including ones I ran.

The highest form of intelligence, he quotes Krishnamurti saying, is to observe without evaluating. I fail this test daily.

Here is the same moment two ways.

**Jackal:** “You’re always late to the standup. It’s disrespectful. You clearly don’t care about the team.”

**NVC:** “The last three standups started without you. I feel frustrated because I need us to be able to plan the day. Would you be willing to join at 9.30, or tell me the day before if you can’t?”

Same data. Completely different conversation.

The first invites a defence. The second invites a yes, a no, or a better idea.

I wish I had this when I sent that test-plan email in my first management job. I wrote an evaluation. I thought I was being clear. I was being violent in the way Rosenberg means it — words that make it harder for the other person to stay in the conversation.

The part that changed my coaching is the need underneath the feeling.

Anger at a missed deadline is rarely about the deadline. It is a need for reliability, or respect, or rest. Once I can name my need, I stop needing them to be the villain.

That is also how I listen now. Not “what is their position?” What are they needing? [Ask by Jeff Wetzler](/post/summary-ask-by-jeff-wetzler) is the modern playbook for questions. Rosenberg is the older, stranger book that explains why the question matters.

A warning. If you recite the four steps like a script, people will feel managed. Translate it into your own mouth. I still say “I need us to…” more than “I have a need for…” Same move. Less incense.

## 3 Big Ideas From Nonviolent Communication by Marshall Rosenberg

**Big Idea 1 — Observation is not evaluation**

Most workplace conflict starts with a smear sold as a fact.

“You hijacked the meeting.”
“That design is sloppy.”
“Finance are blockers.”

Rosenberg’s test: would a camera agree? A camera can show that two people spoke over the agenda item. It cannot show a hijack. The moment you add the verdict, the other person argues the verdict. The data never gets heard.

Leaders who skip this step spend their week in identity conversations. [Difficult Conversations](/post/summary-difficult-conversations) maps that trap. NVC gives you the first move out of it: say only what happened.

**Big Idea 2 — Feelings point to needs, not to other people’s character**

“I feel that you’re not listening” is not a feeling. It is an accusation with “I feel” glued on the front.

A feeling is: frustrated, anxious, disappointed, relieved, proud.

The need is why the feeling is there: clarity, consideration, competence, rest, belonging.

This is the bit leaders skip because it sounds soft. It is the opposite. When I can say “I am anxious because I need a decision before Thursday,” I am being more precise than “you’re dragging your feet.” Precision is kind. Fuzz is where resentment grows.

Rosenberg’s claim is stronger than etiquette. If you cannot tell a feeling from a thought, you will keep trying to change other people instead of making a request.

**Big Idea 3 — A request is not a demand**

A demand has a punishment attached. Tone, status, the next review. The other person can hear it even when you smile.

A request is specific, doable, and allows a no. “Would you be willing to send the risk log by Wednesday?” is a request. “I need you to care more” is a demand dressed as a need. Nobody can do “care more” by Friday.

The test: if they say no, do you punish them? If yes, it was never a request.

This is where NVC meets [Getting to Yes](/post/summary-getting-to-yes-by-roger-fisher-william-ury). Interests, not positions. Needs, not demands. Same family. Rosenberg is more personal. Fisher, Ury and Patton are more commercial. I use both.

Empathy in this book is not nodding. It is guessing the feeling and the need until the other person feels met. Then — and only then — you problem-solve. I still jump to solutions. That is my ruinous empathy in a new costume: I want the discomfort to end. Rosenberg would tell me to stay with the need a minute longer.
