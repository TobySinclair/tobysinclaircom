## Should You Read This?

**Toby's Rating: 8/10**

[Nonviolent Communication](https://www.amazon.com/dp/189200528X) by Marshall Rosenberg is a classic for a reason. Eight million copies. Satya Nadella made it required reading at Microsoft. The four-step model — observation, feeling, need, request — is the cleanest language I know for a hard conversation that doesn’t turn into a prosecution.

It can sound a bit workshop-y. People start sentences with “I’m feeling a need for autonomy” and the room winces. Ignore that bit. Keep the structure. Use it in a one-to-one tomorrow.

If you already have [Fierce Conversations](/post/summary-fierce-conversations-by-susan-scott) and [Radical Candor](/post/summary-radical-candor-by-kim-scott) on the shelf, this is the missing grammar.

## Toby's Top Takeaway From Nonviolent Communication by Marshall Rosenberg

I’m very good at diagnosis.

“They’re not committed.”
“They’re political.”
“They don’t get it.”

Those sentences feel like facts. They’re not. They’re judgements.

Rosenberg calls this jackal language. Blame, labels, shoulds. It’s the default dialect of most leadership meetings I’ve sat in. Including ones I ran.

Here is the same moment two ways.

**Jackal:** “You’re always late to the standup. It’s disrespectful. You clearly don’t care about the team.”

**NVC:** “The last three standups started without you. I feel frustrated because I need us to be able to plan the day. Would you be willing to join at 9.30, or tell me the day before if you can’t?”

Same data. Completely different conversation.

The first invites a defence. The second invites a yes, a no, or a better idea.

The part that changed my coaching is the need underneath the feeling.

Anger at a missed deadline is rarely about the deadline. It’s a need for reliability, or respect, or rest. Once I can name my need, I stop needing them to be the villain.

A warning. If you recite the four steps like a script, people will feel managed. Translate it into your own mouth. I still say “I need us to…” more than “I have a need for…” Same move. Less incense.

## 3 Big Ideas From Nonviolent Communication by Marshall Rosenberg

**1. Observation is not evaluation**

Most workplace conflict starts with a smear sold as a fact.

“You hijacked the meeting.”
“That design is sloppy.”
“Finance are blockers.”

Rosenberg’s test: would a camera agree? A camera can show that two people spoke over the agenda item. It cannot show a hijack. The moment you add the verdict, the other person argues the verdict. The data never gets heard.

Say only what happened. Then stop.

**2. Feelings point to needs**

“I feel that you’re not listening” is not a feeling. It’s an accusation with “I feel” glued on the front.

A feeling is: frustrated, anxious, disappointed, relieved.

The need is why the feeling is there: clarity, consideration, rest, belonging.

This is the bit leaders skip because it sounds soft. It’s the opposite. “I’m anxious because I need a decision before Thursday” is more precise than “you’re dragging your feet.”

If you can’t tell a feeling from a thought, you’ll keep trying to change other people instead of making a request.

**3. A request is not a demand**

A demand has a punishment attached. Tone, status, the next review. The other person can hear it even when you smile.

A request is specific, doable, and allows a no. “Would you be willing to send the risk log by Wednesday?” is a request. “I need you to care more” is not. Nobody can do “care more” by Friday.

The test: if they say no, do you punish them? If yes, it was never a request.

I still jump to solutions too fast. I want the discomfort to end. Rosenberg would tell me to stay with the need a minute longer.
