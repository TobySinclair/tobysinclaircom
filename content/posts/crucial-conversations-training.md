I've written a [summary of Crucial Conversations](/post/summary-crucial-conversations-by-kerry-patterson) that thousands of people read every year. This article is for what happens after you finish the book.

Because here's the uncomfortable pattern I've seen, first as a coach and now as the founder of a conversation-practice platform: people read Crucial Conversations, love it, and change nothing. Six months later they're facing the same conversation they were avoiding when they bought the book, except now they can name the concepts they're failing to use.

The book isn't the problem. The gap between knowing and doing is the problem, and it's a gap only practice closes. So let's look honestly at the training options, what each is actually good for, and what I'd recommend depending on your situation. I run [Real Talk Studio](https://www.realtalkstudio.com), an AI roleplay platform, so I have an obvious interest here. I'll flag where that colours things and where the other options genuinely beat mine.

[Fierce Conversations](/post/summary-fierce-conversations-by-susan-scott) makes the same point from a different angle: the conversation is the relationship. Training that never lets you have one is just vocabulary.

## Option 1: The official Crucial Conversations course

Crucial Learning (the company behind the book) runs official courses, virtual and in-person, typically over several sessions.

**What it's good for.** Shared vocabulary at scale. If a whole leadership team goes through it, "let's get back to dialogue" becomes a phrase people can actually use with each other. The content is faithful to the book and well-facilitated.

**Where it falls short.** It's still mostly instruction. There's some practice built in, but a course with a cohort gives each person minutes of actual conversation time, not hours, and the practice partner is a fellow student being polite. Cost per head is significant, and the skills-fade problem is real: without follow-up reps, most of it evaporates in weeks. That's not a criticism of this course specifically. It's true of every workshop ever run.

## Option 2: Facilitated workshops with actors or coaches

The premium option: professional actors playing your difficult counterpart, or a coach running bespoke role play.

**What it's good for.** This is the gold standard for realism. A good actor gives you the emotional temperature of a real conversation, and a good facilitator debriefs it properly. For very senior people preparing for very high-stakes moments, a board confrontation, a public hearing, this is worth the money.

**Where it falls short.** The money. Actor-based training costs enough that organisations ration it to a handful of leaders, once. And it's an event, not a habit: you get one or two attempts on one day, then never again. The people who most need reps, new managers, frontline teams, get priced out entirely.

If you want a facilitated programme rather than a platform, that's also [the work I do with enterprises](/work-with-me).

## Option 3: Peer practice

Grab a colleague, brief them on your situation, rehearse.

**What it's good for.** It's free, it's available today, and just saying the hard sentence out loud to another human is worth more than another read of the book. If you do only one thing after reading Crucial Conversations, do this.

**Where it falls short.** Two things I see constantly. First, colleagues are too kind. They soften the pushback, and you rehearse against a difficulty level real life won't offer you. Second, the awkwardness tax: practising an emotional conversation in front of a workmate feels exposing, so people quietly don't do it. The method that depends on humans volunteering for awkwardness has a compliance problem built in.

## Option 4: AI roleplay simulation

Full disclosure: this is what I build. An AI character plays your counterpart, stays in character, pushes back, gets emotional, and gives you feedback on what you actually said.

**What it's good for.** Reps. This is the thing none of the other options can offer at any price: the same conversation, attempted ten times, in private, with feedback between attempts. No audience, so the awkwardness tax disappears, and people practise conversations they'd never volunteer to role play in front of colleagues. The characters we build lose composure, challenge you, and can end the call if you break their trust, because consequence is what makes practice feel real. Cost per person is a fraction of any facilitated option, which means everyone gets access, not just the executive layer.

**Where it falls short.** I'll be straight about the limits. An AI counterpart, however good, isn't your actual boss with your actual history. The relationship-specific weight of a real conversation, the years of context, the fact that this person controls your pay review, can't be fully simulated. And AI practice without any human debrief can miss what a skilled coach would catch about your patterns. For the highest-stakes single conversation of someone's career, I'd still add a human coach on top. For building the underlying skill through volume, simulation wins, and it isn't close.

## So what should you actually do?

Depends who you are:

- **You've just read the book and face one specific conversation.** Write the sentence the conversation exists to deliver. Rehearse it out loud, then against resistance: a candid colleague, or [a simulation](https://www.realtalkstudio.com). Don't let your first attempt be the real one. Steal a setup from [these ten manager scenarios](/post/difficult-conversation-scenarios-managers) if you don't want to invent one.
- **You run L&D and you're considering the official course.** Run it if shared vocabulary matters to you, but budget for what comes after, because the course without practice infrastructure is a very expensive book club. Pair it with a practice layer people can use weekly.
- **You have real budget and one senior leader with one enormous conversation coming.** Actor-based rehearsal plus a coach. Genuinely the best option for that narrow case.
- **You want a whole team to actually get better.** Frequency beats intensity. Whatever combination you pick, the design question is "how do people get their tenth rep?", not "how good is day one?".
